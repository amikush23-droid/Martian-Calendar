The Martian Time Keeping

A Proposal

A Martian day is known as a Sol, which is 24 hours, 39 minutes and 35.244 seconds long, or 88,775.244 seconds long. That is almost the same as Earth but it still gives rise newer forms of time keeping in order to make the daily lives of people on Mars easier. Let's explore...

The Martian clock

How Martian Time Is Divided

Seconds on Mars are the same as seconds on Earth. A second on both planets is the same length, as defined by the SI system. Martian time builds on that second with two new units we have defined for Mars: the Chrone and the Archon.

A Chrone is a Martian minute of 355 seconds. An Archon is a Martian hour made of 10 Chrones, which is 3,550 seconds. A full Martian sol is 25 Archons, which is 88,750 seconds on the clock: 25 × 10 × 355 = 88,750. Seconds within each Chrone are numbered 1 to 355.

A real Martian sol lasts 88,775.244 seconds, so each sol runs 25.244 seconds longer than the clock shows. We set this difference aside rather than adding it to every sol, because adding it would break the clock into awkward numbers like 3,551 or 3,698.97 seconds. Keeping the clock on round divisions makes it easy to read, build, and calculate with.

The set-aside time is recovered through leap Chrones. Each leap Chrone adds 355 seconds, one full Chrone, at the end of a sol. Over a cycle of 225 sols, the clock falls behind by 5,679.9 seconds, and 16 leap Chrones add 5,680 seconds. The leap Chrones fall at fixed sols in a repeating pattern: after every 14 sols for 15 intervals, then after 15 sols for one interval, so the cycle has 16 insertions. The pattern never resets between years, and the net error is about 0.1 seconds per 225 sols.

The calendar

How the Martian Year Is Divided

A common Martian year has 668 sols, made of 24 months: 20 months of 28 sols and 4 months of 27 sols. A real Martian year lasts about 668.6 sols, so each year falls 0.6 sols short of the calendar. We set this difference aside rather than adding it every year, because the calendar would otherwise need fractional days. Keeping the year on whole sols keeps the months, weeks, and dates clean.

The set-aside time is recovered through leap years. Every fifth Martian year (Year 005, Year 010, and so on) is a leap year of 671 sols. Over five years the calendar falls behind by 3.0 sols (5 × 0.6), and the three leap days repay exactly that. In a leap year, the months Fenrir, Lilith and Raavan, the 6th, 12th and 18th months, each gain a 28th sol, while Zephyrus, the 24th month, keeps its 27 sols. The five-year cycle totals 3,343 sols: four common years of 668 plus one leap year of 671. The seven-sol week runs straight through every year and leap year without resetting.

The Martian seasons

Orbital eccentricity & axial tilt

Why Martian Seasons Are Unequal

Mars has an axial tilt of 25.19°, very close to Earth’s 23.44°, giving the planet four distinct seasons. However, Mars’s orbit is markedly elliptical (eccentricity 0.0934, over five times Earth’s).

To track where Mars is along its orbit, scientists measure its solar longitude (Ls)—an orbital angle from 0° to 360° that works like a giant 360° compass around the Sun. One full Martian year is a complete 360° circle, split into four equal 90° angular quarters for the four seasons (0°–90°, 90°–180°, 180°–270°, and 270°–360°). By Kepler’s second law, a planet moves slower when it is farther from the Sun and faster when it is closer—so Mars travels slowest near aphelion (Ls 71°, its farthest point from the Sun) and fastest near perihelion (Ls 251°, its closest point).

As a result, even though every season sweeps through the exact same 90° angle in space, the four astronomical quarters of solar longitude (Ls) last for sharply different numbers of sols: Northern Spring (194 sols) and Summer (178 sols) are long and mild, while Northern Autumn (142 sols) and Winter (154 sols)—which is Southern Summer—are short, fast, and intensely heated.

How the weekday moves

Weekday drift

The 35-Year Martian Weekday Cycle

The Martian week has 7 sols (Sun, Mon, Tue, Wed, Thu, Fri, Sat), matching Earth’s 7-day naming convention, running continuously without resetting between months or years.

A common Martian year has 668 sols. Dividing 668 by 7 gives 95 full weeks plus a remainder of 3 sols (668 = 95 × 7 + 3). Each common year therefore shifts the starting sol of the year (Artemis, Sol 1) forward by 3 weekdays.

Every fifth Martian year is a leap year of 671 sols (671 = 95 × 7 + 6), advancing the calendar forward by 6 weekdays—which is equivalent to drifting 1 day backward.

Combining the 5-year leap cycle with the 7-sol week requires 5 × 7 = 35 Martian years (23,401 sols) for the cycle to repeat completely. Across 500 Martian years (334,300 sols), this 35-year cycle repeats 14 full times plus 10 years, preserving seamless day-of-week tracking across centuries.

Translating Between Martian & Earth Time

Every calendar date on Mars maps to a unique Absolute Sol Number counted continuously from Year 001, Artemis, Sol 1. Because four common years of 668 sols and one leap year of 671 sols form an exact 3,343-sol five-year cycle, converting between calendar dates and total elapsed sols is completely deterministic.

Bridging Martian and Earth durations relies on the shared SI second: one mean Martian solar day lasts 88,775.244 SI seconds compared to Earth’s 86,400 SI seconds, making each Martian sol equal to approximately 1.02749125 Earth days (24 hours, 39 minutes, 35.244 seconds).
